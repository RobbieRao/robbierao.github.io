## Design the openings as well as the shell

We built four layers with separate lighting spaces and light for the structure as a whole. Small openings corresponded to point sources; longer cuts followed the LED strips. I read the gaps between the layers as part of the design too, because they determine how much light escapes beyond the pattern.

The model used 5 mm acrylic and mirror paper. The early contour was rough, so we used mirror paper to adjust its appearance and soften the visible light. Musical-note shapes near the center picked up halos from the internal point sources. Here, material finishing changed the light people saw, as well as the outline of the object.

## Keep the color when changing the rhythm

The Unity panel separates modes from parameters. Single selects one color, Rainbow changes the color pattern and Breath varies brightness rhythmically. UniOSC carries the controls. The large touch area handles HSB adjustment, while breathing frequency has a separate control.

That separation is useful when judging the assembled light. Once a color feels right, changing the pace should not force another color choice. We tested the single-color, rainbow and breathing states on the assembled units. In the interface sheet, the control and the resulting physical light sit beside each other, so the setting has a visible consequence to compare.

## Give each moving unit a place on the panel

The motion panel uses the numbered arrangement of the nine units, with rise and descend controls for each. A motor drives a telescopic rod inside the mechanism. Matching a control to a unit makes it possible to work on one part of the arrangement without treating the installation as a single moving object.

Stop holds the present arrangement; Reset returns the units to their reference positions. I keep those actions separate when checking the mechanism. A partially raised module changes the overlap of the cut shells and the light passing through them. Holding it there gives time to inspect that particular pattern before returning the whole structure.

## Leave room for the cables to move

During movement tests, neighboring modules needed gaps to avoid rubbing and collisions. The wiring needed freedom to travel with them. We tried a connected strip to reduce the load, then had to deal with tangling during assembly. Packing the units closer would have made both problems harder to see and reach.

An exposed strip could illuminate a seam or cable instead of the intended cut pattern. The assembly made me see the light controls and mechanism together: a color setting did not determine the visible pattern on its own. The shell opening, cable position and height of the module also shaped it. Stop and Reset were useful for different reasons: one retained a position for inspection; the other restored the reference arrangement.
