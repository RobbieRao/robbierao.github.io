Blind Box Experience Upgrade

A Dunhuang-inspired character and experience system connecting collectible figures, grotto-inspired packaging, face-capture exploration, and a mini program for opening and keeping digital items.

Year
2021
Practice
Product · interaction · cultural experience
My role
Technical implementation · collaborative 3D modeling

Designing beyond the reveal

I handled the technical implementation and worked with the team on 3D modeling for this Dunhuang-inspired blind-box project. The team developed two character families, packaging, a proposed experience venue, and digital interfaces under the Hey Dundun identity. My part sat between the models and their use: a character had to work as an object, appear consistently in the interface, and become something a visitor could interact with.

I approach the experience as a sequence: understand a series, choose and purchase, open the box, recognize the character, then keep or share it. Each step needs different information. Before purchase, the series and possible characters matter; at the reveal, the result must be unmistakable; afterward, the name and cultural reference give the object something to sustain attention beyond the initial surprise.

The two series offer different ways into that sequence. The flying-musician figures are organized around instruments and performance poses. The auspicious-animal series uses the nine-colored deer, blue bird, winged horse, and treasure-guarding dragon as its starting points. Packaging displays the family together, while the individual cards concentrate on one named character. That change of scale—from the group to the individual—is useful when moving from browsing a series to recognizing a result.

The useful question for me is what remains after the package is opened. A figure, a card, an account record, and a possible encounter with an animated version should refer to the same character. If each surface invents its own name, crop, or visual emphasis, the experience becomes harder to follow even though all the assets are attractive on their own.

Keeping the character legible in 3D

I contributed to the models alongside the team’s illustrators and other modelers. A drawing can carry identity through linework and a carefully chosen view; a small 3D figure needs that identity to survive changes in angle. I use the instrument, headdress, silhouette, and large color regions as separate checks when judging whether the modeled character still reads clearly.

In the musician series, the objects held by the characters do real visual work: Weiqing has a pipa, Yun Que a konghou, Lingmai a ruan, and Zangzhu a pellet drum. Their proportions and pose relationships distinguish the figures before a viewer reads the names. The pipa model shows why the space between hands, instrument, clothing, and body matters. Those forms need to remain distinguishable rather than merging into one dense shape from the main viewing angle.

The animal family shifts the emphasis toward the head and body outline. The deer’s antlers, the winged horse’s upward forms and side wings, and the dragon’s facial features provide larger identification cues. Surface ornament then adds specificity. This order is helpful in a small product image: the outline and color grouping must survive before fine patterning can contribute.

The accompanying card is another scale test. It places the character inside a repeatable graphic frame with a name, a series title, and decorative elements. A rendered model and an illustrated card do not need identical lighting or texture, but they need enough shared cues to be recognized as the same figure. I treat that continuity as part of preparing a model for use, rather than considering the render an endpoint.

Packaging as a small display space

The team explored a packaging structure based on the square plan, niches, and truncated-pyramid ceiling associated with a grotto interior. The design studies move between a flat net, an open enclosure, and a roof-like top. This gives the cultural reference a structural role: it changes the shape and opening of the container, as well as its printed surface.

I read the red net together with the white volume studies. The net explains where the faces and folds meet; the volume shows what those decisions do to the opening and interior. The arched recesses create a place for the figure to be seen, while the base establishes a small stage. It is a more useful way to discuss the box than showing only a closed-package render.

The printed variations extract flowing bands and color relationships from mural imagery. Their application is shown on several packaging and display forms. What I check across those views is where the pattern lands at an edge, how it behaves around an opening, and whether a strong color region competes with the figure. A flat pattern that looks balanced can be cut very differently once it wraps around a volume.

These are structural and visual design studies. To move toward fabrication, I would check the figure’s clearance, the order of opening, card placement, and protection of protruding instruments or headdresses on a physical sample. Those checks follow directly from the modeled forms: a visually open display and a protective shipping package have to be resolved in the same object.

From a static model to a responding character

The experience-venue proposal adds a spatial encounter with the characters. The circular model organizes activity around a central opening and includes projected imagery; the section studies show the relationship between the central feature and surrounding circulation. The space was a team design. My technical focus was how a visitor’s action could reach a virtual character within that setting.

The face-capture experiment used Unreal Engine 4 with iPhone X ARKit blendshapes. The development photographs show the capture view beside the character in the engine. This is a useful technical boundary: the camera supplies changing facial information, while the character needs deformation controls that can represent it. A finished-looking static mesh alone does not supply that connection.

I separate character selection from character animation. The proposed preference questionnaire narrows a library of clothing, headwear, expressions, and colors; facial information can be used to match available features. Animation then changes an already selected character in response to movement. Keeping those operations distinct makes the design easier to reason about: choosing an appearance and driving a face require different inputs and different feedback.

For the capture work, I would inspect neutral appearance and simple movements separately before putting them into a richer scene. Eye and mouth motion need to remain legible on the character’s proportions; a larger projection also makes small mismatches more visible. The prototype is valuable here because it exposes the handoff from capture to deformation, where the original illustration, the 3D model, and the animation controls have to agree.

The larger venue and personalized matching remain design proposals around the working capture experiment. I would develop them by testing one short encounter first: select a character, see a clear response to an action, and leave with an identifiable record of that encounter. That would connect the body-based interaction back to the character someone browsed or opened earlier.

Purchase, reveal, and the collection record

During this project, I learned to make a WeChat mini program, use WeChat Developer Tools, and set up a server. The interface gave me a concrete reason to learn each part: a visitor needed to move from browsing a series to choosing a box, seeing a character, and finding it again in the cabinet. I was working out how to turn those screens into a connected experience.

I separate the product being browsed, the box selected for opening, and the character shown after the reveal. They need different information. The product view introduces a series; the selection state identifies the box being acted on; the result names a particular character. That distinction helps me decide what a button changes and what information the next page needs to receive.

The development screenshot places the cabinet preview beside its WXML source. Learning to use WeChat Developer Tools gave me a way to work between that page structure and the visible result. For a cabinet like this, I look at the empty view, the view with an item, and the route into the item detail separately. A useful check is to follow one character through those views: its image, name, and destination should stay in agreement as the page changes.

Learning server setup also made me pay attention to the boundary between the interface and the data it depends on. A highlighted box is a temporary selection; a revealed character is something the visitor expects to find again. When thinking through that connection, I ask what information the page sends, what response it needs, and what it should show while it is waiting. An empty collection, a request still in progress, and a confirmed result need different feedback; otherwise the same blank space can mean several things to the user.

The reveal overlay centers the character card, names the result, and provides a route to the cabinet. The gifting interface has a separate explanation and confirmation step. These transitions deserve the same attention as the animation: a person should know what they now have, where to find it, and whether they are sharing a picture or transferring an item.

Looking back at the screens, I would reduce the competition between “reveal again,” undo-style actions, and viewing the result. After a reveal, I want the character and its story to have some room. Repeated purchase prompts take attention away from that encounter. This is the part of the interface I would revise first: make inspecting the result an obvious next step, then leave the other actions available without giving them equal emphasis.

Now, when I look at a collection interface, I follow the information through it: where the character comes from, which action changes the current state, and how the visitor gets back to the result. The modeling work taught me to keep a character recognizable across views. Making the mini program and learning server setup extended that concern into the sequence of actions around it. Those are skills I gained here alongside the 3D and face-capture work.
