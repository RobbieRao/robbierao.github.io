The Lost Homeland

Corridor and fragment-field environments

For The Lost Homeland, we built the data-universe setting around two contrasting environments. The dark circular corridor enclosed the view along a continuous route. The cyan architectural fragments left open space between pieces, exposing light and depth. We used these different spatial organizations to support the film's theme of belonging.

I first compare these scenes at the scale of the whole shot. The ring has a central opening and a surrounding enclosure; the fragment field breaks the enclosure into separate elements. Material detail and moving effects need to fit those larger shapes, otherwise the two environments lose their spatial distinction.

Layered ring geometry and lighting zones

We organized the ring in concentric layers and placed room volumes around its circumference. Inside a room, we retained a flat floor for furniture; elsewhere in the ring, we reserved lighting areas. This established where objects could stand and where illumination could enter before we developed the surfaces.

Room volume, floor, and lighting areas could then be adjusted separately within the ring. In the darker views, the enclosure dominated; in the brighter view, the central opening established the depth. I check the opening and its surrounding silhouette together, because the same geometry can read very differently when that light balance changes.

Geometry, materials, and event graphs

Our workflow used Blender for modeling and material views, with Unreal Engine graphs for surface construction and scene behavior. The material function combined texture sampling with numerical operations. A separate BP_FirstPersonCharacter event graph handled behavior. Geometry, materials, and events therefore had distinct editing contexts.

The large geometry determined the opening and silhouette. Surface treatment changed how that geometry responded to light, so I compare the rendered surface with the material network before considering a layout change. The event graph concerns another problem: how scene states and behavior change over time.

For this enclosed scene, I distinguish a geometry problem from a lighting or material problem by checking the opening first. If its outline is unclear, the ring and room volumes need attention. If the outline reads but the surrounding surface disappears, I compare the illumination and material response before changing the layout. The reserved lighting zones make that separation possible: the floor can remain available for furniture while illumination enters elsewhere. Looking at the bright-opening view beside the dark corridor also shows how much of the enclosure comes from geometry and how much comes from the distribution of light.

Fluid study and shot composition

We also explored a blue-and-white fluid effect in TouchDesigner. The fluid1 controls exposed pressure, velocity diffusion, density diffusion, buoyancy, and curl separately. We kept the output visible beside the network while adjusting those controls, so a parameter change could be compared with the resulting motion.

For the corridor shot, I look at the central opening, bright interior, and darker surrounding structure first. For the fragment field, I look at the gaps between pieces. Those relationships establish the depth; I judge the finer surface and motion detail against them.
