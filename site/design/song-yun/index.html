Song-Yun Culture AIGC

An AR cultural experience connecting pavilion stories, stone inscriptions and small rituals along a walk through Wushan.

Year
2022
Practice
Cultural interaction · AR
My role
Collaborative interaction and cultural-content design

Keep the pavilion in the first view

At Siyi Pavilion, the proposal brings a Song-style character into the camera view to introduce the place. I kept coming back to the first screen: the visitor has already walked to the pavilion, so the building needs room. In the landscape layout, the character stays to one side and the pavilion opening remains visible. The narration can point to something the visitor is actually looking at.

The character, inscription and camera view were competing for the same small screen. Keeping the character to one side left the building legible and made the scene-specific narration plausible. The first three stops also involved physical markers. In this proposal, the visitor’s position and the page composition were connected before any character animation was considered.

Read the couplet before the explanation

The six columns at Siyi Pavilion carry three couplets. “放怀听流水，小坐数行云” already contains actions: listening, sitting and looking. I treated those words as a reason to look back at the place. They gave this stop more specific material than a general introduction to Song culture could have done.

The portrait studies separated the original inscription from the character and interpretation. That gave the reading two levels: recognize the paired lines first, then approach their explanation. The columns and couplets remained the reference for both layouts, so changing the presentation did not have to change the subject being read.

Working through these layouts made me distinguish image generation from content editing. Character appearance allows visual exploration; a line break or the order of a couplet affects meaning. A polished character image cannot settle those textual decisions. The reading sequence carries as much of the cultural content as the visual style does.

Let the wish have a readable ending

The wishing-pool proposal changed the visitor’s role. After looking at buildings and reading inscriptions, the visitor shook a fortune-stick container and received a blessing. We placed the result slip in the center at the largest scale, giving this stop a clear visual destination.

What stayed with me was the difference between the movement and its result. The shaking supplied anticipation, but the words on the slip were what the visitor received. The final composition therefore mattered as much as the motion imagined before it. This was a small interaction within the route, with a different pace from the explanatory stops.

Save the largest motion for the last stop

At Fanghe Pavilion, the proposal paired fireworks with “走向吴山，点亮杭城.” This stop closed the route with a larger visual gesture after the smaller acts of observing, reading and drawing a wish. It did not introduce another dense passage of explanation.

Putting the stops beside one another made the role of that ending clearer to me. A firework scene on its own is an effect; at the end of this sequence, it changes the pace and marks completion. The route gave the animation its reason to be there.

Landscape characters and portrait inscriptions

The proposal combined physical markers, place-specific text and media, and a sequence for presenting them. The technical exploration included WeChat xr-frame. The landscape character views and portrait inscription studies already showed that a single placement rule would not serve all the content.

The layouts made the relationship between the physical site and the phone more concrete for me. A character needed room beside a building, a couplet needed a readable order, and a wish needed a place for its result. Those were different composition problems even though all of them belonged to the same AR proposal.
