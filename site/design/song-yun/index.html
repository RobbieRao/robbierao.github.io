Song-Yun Culture AIGC

An AR cultural experience connecting pavilion stories, stone inscriptions and small rituals along a walk through Wushan.

Year
2022
Practice
Cultural interaction · AR
My role
Collaborative interaction and cultural-content design

Keep the pavilion in view

The first stop is Siyi Pavilion. The proposal brings a Song-style guide into the camera view to introduce the pavilion. I keep returning to the composition of that first screen: a visitor has already made the effort to stand here, so the architecture needs room. In the landscape layout, the guide sits to the side and the opening of the pavilion remains visible. The explanation belongs beside the place being explained.

A character, a paragraph and the live camera feed all compete for attention. I would give them different jobs: the character establishes who is speaking, the short text identifies what to look at, and audio carries the longer explanation. Keeping the whole script on screen would ask someone to read while also holding and aiming a phone. The useful question is how much a visitor can take in before lowering it.

The proposal calls for physical markers at the first three stops. That makes the sign itself part of the interface. A useful sign needs to explain where to stand and what scanning will reveal; the loading state then needs to confirm that the correct stop has been found. I would test that complete arrival sequence on site before polishing the guide’s animation.

Two ways to read an inscription

Siyi Pavilion’s six columns carry three couplets. That gives this stop much more specific material than a generic introduction to Song culture. “放怀听流水，小坐数行云” already suggests a way of inhabiting the pavilion: listen, sit, look. My interest is in helping a visitor connect those words to what they can see and hear at the site.

The portrait proposals explore two presentations: a pair of vertical inscription panels and a guide with an explanatory text area. I read them as two moments in the same visit. First let someone see the original words in their paired order; then offer an explanation. Showing both at full intensity immediately would conceal the column the visitor is trying to read.

The content structure needs a stable pairing between the physical columns, the original text and the explanation. A line break or a swapped pair is not merely a typographic issue: it changes what the visitor thinks belongs to that location. I would keep these as separately editable fields, check them against the site, and let the interface reuse the same verified text in both orientations.

The character style can be explored through generated imagery, but the interpretation of an inscription needs its own editorial pass. I do not want a persuasive visual style to lend false authority to an imprecise explanation. For me, this is where cultural interaction design becomes an editing task as much as an image-making task.

A small ritual with a clear ending

At the wishing-pool rock, the proposed interaction is a shaking fortune-stick container followed by a blessing. It changes the pace of the walk: after looking at architecture and reading inscriptions, the visitor gets to anticipate a result. The content can stay light—peace, good fortune, family happiness—because the point is the moment of participation.

I separate this into three observable states: the scene is ready, the draw is happening, and the result can be read. The original result proposal makes the blessing the largest element in the phone view. I would retain that hierarchy. Once the draw finishes, motion should settle long enough for someone to read or photograph the result.

A repeat action needs a definite rule. If someone taps twice during the shake, does it restart, queue another draw, or do nothing? If tracking disappears, does the result survive? My preference is to let one draw complete and keep the result available until the visitor chooses to draw again. These small rules determine whether the interaction feels deliberate or unreliable.

Let the final stop close the walk

The Fanghe Pavilion stop is planned around layered fireworks, followed by the line “走向吴山，点亮杭城”. This has a different role from the earlier stops. It does not need another dense explanation; it needs to give the walk a recognizable finish. I would reserve the most expansive motion for this moment so the route has a change in scale.

“Several fireworks” still leaves an important timing decision open. If every burst and the closing line arrive together, the visitor cannot tell what to follow. I would stagger the bursts, allow the final layer to recede, and then hold the words. The animation timeline should therefore carry an editorial decision about reading, not just a collection of effects.

For an outdoor phone experience, I would check the closing screen against bright sky and foliage as well as a dark preview background. Contrast, the visible area of the architecture and the time someone has to hold a phone all matter here. A scene can look convincing on a workstation yet be difficult to follow at the actual stop.

What I would keep separate in implementation

Looking across the stops, the recurring structure is straightforward: a physical target selects a place, that place supplies its text and media, and a small sequence determines what appears next. I would keep those three responsibilities separate. A revised inscription should not require changes to tracking code, and a character asset should not contain the logic that decides which location is active.

The technical research materials include WeChat’s xr-frame route. The questions I would settle before committing to a runtime are practical: how the target is acquired, what happens when tracking is lost, which layers stay fixed to the screen, and which belong to the scene. The portrait text panels and landscape guide proposal already expose why one placement rule will not work for every element.

My next iteration would use a simple field test sheet for each stop: arrival and scan, first useful content, text legibility, audio control, loss and recovery of tracking, and exit. That would produce more actionable feedback than asking only whether the AR looks impressive.
