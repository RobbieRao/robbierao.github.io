Keeping a reference as the motif changes

Loomed Zenith brought Liangzhu motifs into generated imagery, digital garments, and a physical exhibition. I worked on the AI workflow, interaction design, hardware programming, models, and scenes. Zheng Jiaqi led motif digitization and display-object design; Wang Ziqi developed the garment concepts, models, and textures. My part connected this visual research to a system that could be adjusted and exhibited.

The library includes deity-and-beast masks, jade birds, fish, and cloud-scroll forms. Simple contours, complex motifs, and recomposed garment bands were kept as distinct groups. Each gives me a different basis for comparison: the axis and internal divisions of a mask, the direction and rhythm of a scroll, or the connection between repeats in a band. An antique-looking surface alone does not establish a relationship to the source motif.

The comparison changes again when a flat motif becomes an object. Our display places a sculptural form inside a transparent case above its flat motif. A fine line seen from the front can become an occlusion from the side; an empty area can become an opening or a shadow. I want the source and the volume available together so that the change of medium remains visible.

Separating the decisions inside ComfyUI

In ComfyUI, I separated the question of how a motif should change into several decisions. The reference image provides the structural starting point, text conditioning describes the direction, and a separate palette controls color. Keeping these inputs distinct makes a variation easier to judge. If structure, color, and detail are all buried in one prompt, it becomes difficult to explain what a selected image actually preserves.

The graph encodes the reference through a VAE, supplies positive and negative SDXL conditions, and samples in latent space with an Advanced KSampler. A separate Refiner stage precedes the final VAE decode. I treat structural judgment and detail judgment separately: first the organization of the motif, then its refined lines, surface, and color. More detail does not automatically make an output more suitable.

The node graph also became part of the display. It offers a path through reference, conditioning, sampling, and output without requiring someone to understand an entire diffusion model first. My practical takeaway is to connect each exposed control to a design decision. A palette explains color choices; an array explains arrangement. A parameter that adds complexity without helping a choice does not need to become an interaction control.

Eight sliders and three arrangements

For the exhibition interaction, I wanted more than entering a prompt and waiting for an image. The table follows the workflow from left to right. Physical sliders adjust the proportions of eight colors; three buttons select rectangular, circular, or random arrangements. Sliders suit a continuous adjustment and buttons suit distinct alternatives, so the action itself helps explain the kind of choice being made.

The panel names eight colors: Baoyu Zhu, Bi Qing, Tan Dao Hei, Yu Qin Huang, Ji Gu Bai, Wu Hui, Ni Tao Fen, and Cong Huang. These are separate color proportions rather than eight preset filters. They turn a vague request for a Liangzhu-related appearance into specific choices: the balance of warm color, the use of dark tones for outlines or background, and the space that pale tones leave around a dense motif.

Array controls address a different problem: how one motif becomes a surface. A rectangular grid emphasizes repeat and spacing; a circular arrangement establishes a center; a random arrangement breaks the regular rhythm. The hardware and image workflow need to agree on these meanings. Buttons change organization, while sliders change color conditions. What I value here is the clear relationship between an action and a visible change, rather than the number of controls.

When the motif becomes a garment

Garments turn a motif into a surface with direction, folds, and a bodily scale. The team used Style3D for pattern pieces, sewing, fabric, and texture settings, drawing on Tang, Song, and Ming silhouettes for new designs. Combining Liangzhu motifs with these silhouettes let us explore how traditional graphics could take shape in contemporary digital garments.

I read the collar, front opening, cuffs, and hem as different surfaces. A narrow border calls for a continuous band; a broad panel needs a decision about density. Fine internal lines may crowd together on a cuff, while a larger repeat can lose its main figure across a skirt. The garment studies retain front, side, back, and detail views, allowing the placement to be judged beyond one attractive camera angle.

My modeling and scene work connected these garments to their presentation environment. The project used Unreal Engine 5 for a rotating three-dimensional garment visualization. Reflections, sky, and ground affect how the garments read: pale fabric needs tonal variation, gold motifs need to remain visible through highlights, and broad sleeves and long hems need a legible silhouette. Scene work has to make the motifs readable as well as create an atmosphere.

Two different reasons to use motion

Motion takes two paths in this project. The ComfyUI branch sends an image into SVD conditioning, samples and decodes a frame sequence, then applies RIFE interpolation and video combination. The other path presents digital garments in an Unreal Engine 5 scene. One explores an image changing over time; the other reveals garment volume, motif placement, and material in space.

I judge these outputs differently. A motif animation needs continuity across neighboring frames, not only strong still images; interpolation adds intermediate frames but does not settle whether the transformation makes sense. A garment scene needs to keep patterns on the opening, sides, and hem legible as the view changes. Distinguishing the paths also tells me whether a problem belongs to image generation, garment texturing, or scene presentation.

The useful method is to decide what motion should reveal before choosing the generation or rendering tool. A transforming motif, a fabric surface, and a group of garments in space require different distances and durations. Giving them all the same camera movement would obscure those differences.

Connecting objects, screens, and garments

The project was realized in a physical exhibition. The installation photographs show a scene across horizontal screens, a portrait display that preserves garment proportions, and transparent tabletop cases pairing sculptural motifs with gold-on-black reference plates. These provide different scales of viewing: the overall scene from a distance, contours and details at the table, then the application of a motif to a garment on screen.

This made the purpose of each exhibit clearer to me. Flat motifs establish a source; physical forms expose a change of medium; the generation workflow explains what can be adjusted; garments show those decisions on a complete object. My interaction and scene work needed to connect these parts so that the display could communicate more than a collection of attractive images.

Looking back, the most useful skills sit at these handoffs: translating motif research into generation conditions, connecting a physical action to a visual decision, and placing garment assets in an appropriate viewing environment. For a future project, I would keep source motifs, generated versions, garment views, and exhibition images together. That would make revisions easier to trace back to the same object instead of relying on memory.
