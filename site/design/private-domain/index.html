Private Domain UX Design

Designing the campus-service journey from discovery and ordering to support across WeChat channels.

Year
2021–2022
Practice
Service design · Community operations
My role
Project framing, service-model planning, and operating strategy.

Start with the task on campus

BIZZLE began with a broad ambition to connect campus life with services outside the university. We used the China Academy of Art as the pilot and focused on delivery, printing, and shopping. During periods of restricted campus access, students depended more heavily on local services. The design problem became quite concrete: where can someone find the service, which campus does it cover, and what happens after they open it?

The pilot interfaces put campus or location selection at the top. Below it, the menus grouped supermarket orders, campus dining, takeaway, printing, and art-material purchasing. Other screens added errands, parcel collection, a secondhand market, lost-and-found, and campus discussion. Those are different intentions, even though they can all fit under campus life. Someone trying to print a file needs a short route; someone browsing a discussion can spend longer exploring.

I organize the entry around three levels: confirm the campus, choose the task, and find support. The campus selector establishes whether a service applies; printing and delivery then receive direct entry points. Community discussion and promotional content have their own place, while order information and help remain easy to recover. The entry-hierarchy study makes those priorities visible before the operating model becomes more complicated.

Include the route back to support

We separated the service provider from the campus-facing operation. The operating model connected Ele.me campus delivery and self-service printing with students, while BIZZLE handled campus announcements, support, content, and community management. This distinction matters to the experience: the student may discover a service through us even when another organization supplies part of it.

I follow the route in both directions. On the way in, a student encounters an announcement or service entry and proceeds to the relevant mini program. On the way back, they need a recognizable channel for information and support. The blueprint keeps the delivery and printing branches separate, then reconnects both to campus notices, instructions, and after-sales help. That return route is part of the service, not an item to add after the entry has been designed.

The two services also have different operating relationships. Delivery connects through a commercial partnership; the printing branch is marked as a direct operation. I keep that distinction when discussing responsibility. A shared campus brand can make discovery simpler, but it does not make every service's fulfillment and support identical. The interface and the operating team need to tell the same story about who handles the next step.

Give each WeChat channel a job

The plan used an official account, campus-specific WeChat groups, mini programs, and a website. I see different jobs in these channels. An official-account article can explain a service and remain available for reference. A mini program can present service choices and order navigation. A campus group gives announcements and questions a local context. Repeating the same promotional message everywhere would leave much of that structure unused.

For a delivery or printing announcement, the important connection is between the explanation and the usable service entry. A reader should not have to search a discussion history to recover the basic instructions. The group can handle changing local information; the stable entry and explanation need a place people can find again. I include content organization in the service-design work so those instructions and entries have a clear place from the start.

The phased plan began with establishing the channels and publishing the first campus content. Later stages added activities and service categories, then recurring content and operating analysis. That sequence gives each addition a maintenance cost. A new service needs more than an icon: its explanation, announcements, and support route have to remain usable across the channels where students encounter it.

Students and partners want different things

We distinguished campus audiences from commercial partners. Students and staff wanted useful services, offers, creative inspiration, and people to exchange with. Service providers, suppliers, and advertisers wanted access to a stable campus audience. Writing both sides down made the conflict easier to see: a partner may want more exposure at the same moment a student wants less interruption and a faster route to a task.

For me, that is a question of placement and timing. A promotion beside a relevant service can help someone make a choice. The same promotion placed before every task makes the service harder to reach. Campus discussion and lost-and-found also need space to function without becoming another sales surface. I use the immediate purpose of each area to judge what belongs there.

I organize operating analysis around three questions: how people discover a service, whether they complete the task, and how they obtain support. Registrations and activity describe reach and use; service completion and support requests describe the quality of the journey. Keeping these measures separate makes a review useful for a specific decision about navigation, content, or service responsibility.

The design work produced a set of connected artifacts: campus-service interfaces, a model of delivery and support responsibilities, and a phased operating plan. Reading them together connects a visible entry point with the organization that fulfills the service and the channels that maintain it. This is the service-design method I take from BIZZLE: develop the interface, content, and operating model together.
