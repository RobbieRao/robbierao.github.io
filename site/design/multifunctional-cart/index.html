Multifunctional Cart

An ergonomic shopping-cart design study developed through posture analysis, structural drawings, 3D modeling, and hands-on prototyping.

从超市购物中的负重、转向与休息需求出发，结合姿态分析、结构草图、三维建模与实体样机，对多功能购物车进行人体工学设计探索。

Year
2021
Practice
Ergonomics · Industrial design

Follow the shopping trip through the body

We began with the tiring parts of shopping: walking for a long time, carrying a bag, finding a place to rest, turning a loaded cart and removing items at checkout. Narrow aisles, rough ground and crowds change what those actions demand. This kept the brief tied to a person moving through a shop rather than to a list of extra cart features.

Our posture study separated Turn, Push, Lift and Sit, which gave the parts different jobs. Handles belonged to pushing and turning, the tray and opening to reaching for objects, and the seat and backrest to resting. Putting these studies beside the cart made the shared space apparent: the same frame had to accommodate the person and the goods in more than one posture.

The joint has to work between the parts

The connecting-rod drawing specifies arm dimensions of 180 and 190, pivot holes of diameter 12 and a thickness of 5. The bends are marked 135 ± 0.5° and 145.1 ± 0.5°. Together, the lengths, angles and holes locate the pivots; the side view shows the thickness occupied by the connection. These numbers describe the relationship between parts, beyond the appearance of the individual rod.

The backrest carries a width annotation of 403 ± 0.2; the cushion curves include R115.29 and R139.30. These are drawing dimensions, not measurements of the wooden prototype. Comparing the views made clear to me what each drawing could answer: a surface radius describes a shape, while clearance depends on the neighboring parts as well.

Use each model to answer a different question

We used the digital model to correct perspective and detail, a test model to adjust mass distribution and balance, and carpentry tools to make the solid-wood prototype. Moving between these models made weight and thickness much less abstract. The clean surfaces of the digital model could not communicate those properties on their own.

The assembled object brought the seat support, tray and front connections into one crowded region. Side and front views showed different overlaps and gaps, while the storage behind the seat added to the overall volume. Separate part drawings had made each component clear; the prototype showed what happened when all of them occupied the frame together.

One frame for carrying and resting

The combined seat and storage returned me to the shopping sequence. At checkout, the opening and tray provide access to the goods; during a rest, the seat, backrest and supports become the main contact points. The design had placed both situations on one frame. Their connection was the actual difficulty of the brief.

The completed prototype gave that connection a physical scale. It also changed what I took a finished model to mean. Making the parts fit demonstrates an arrangement; it does not establish a load rating or a comfort result. The useful outcome of this exercise was being able to see and handle the layout, and to distinguish that achievement from performance we had not measured.
