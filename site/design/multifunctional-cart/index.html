Multifunctional Cart

An ergonomic shopping-cart design study developed through posture analysis, structural drawings, 3D modeling, and hands-on prototyping.

从超市购物中的负重、转向与休息需求出发，结合姿态分析、结构草图、三维建模与实体样机，对多功能购物车进行人体工学设计探索。

Year
2021

Ergonomics · Industrial design

Shopping tasks and posture analysis

We began with situations that make shopping tiring: leg fatigue, bags loading the shoulders, difficulty finding a place to rest, resistance when turning, and repeated unloading at checkout. We also considered narrow aisles, rough ground, crowds, and cart noise. These gave us specific actions and clearances to design around.

We separated Turn, Push, Lift, and Sit in the posture study. Turning involved arm force and a division between supporting and pushing legs. Lifting required the arms to reach forward while the torso leaned slightly back. Sitting brought the seat and back support into the same study. We combined storage, handles, a seat, and a backrest around those different actions.

Component geometry and dimensions

We drew the connecting rod, cushion, tray, and backrest separately. The rod drawing specified arm dimensions of 180 and 190, diameter-12 pivot holes, and a side-view thickness of 5. The bends were marked 135 ± 0.5° and 145.1 ± 0.5°. These dimensions positioned the pivots within the part's outline.

The backrest was dimensioned at 403 ± 0.2 wide, with front, side, and top views. We also used multiple views for the cushion and tray, so thickness and side profiles could be checked against the overall sketches. The dimensions belonged to the drawings; the model and wooden prototype were where we examined the physical fit.

I read the rod dimensions as a set. The 180 and 190 dimensions set the two main lengths, while the bend angles and hole positions complete the pivot geometry. A matching hole diameter alone does not establish a workable joint. The 5-thick side profile, neighboring supports, and space for rotation also matter when the pieces are assembled. I compare the drawing with the prototype at those interfaces rather than judging the mechanism only from its overall silhouette.

The cushion also has a shaped side profile, with radii labeled R115.29 and R139.30. Its curved section and the backrest’s multiple views describe surfaces that are hard to infer from the perspective sketch. The physical prototype adds material thickness and joints to that geometry. It can expose interference and occupied space, while comfort and load capacity still require their own measurements.

Modeling, balance trial, and fabrication

We first used the digital model to correct the sketch's perspective and details. The test model then let us adjust physical mass attributes for balance. Finally, we made the solid-wood prototype by hand with carpentry tools. Each step exposed a different part of the assembly: geometry, mass distribution, then joints and material thickness.

The assembled prototype brought the seat, backrest, storage, wheel supports, and exposed joints into one object. From the front, I can inspect the seat support with the front structure. From the side, I can see how much space the storage behind the seat occupies. Those views make clearances and thickness easier to judge than the component drawings alone.

Storage and seating layout

We placed the seat, backrest, tray, and storage in one wheeled frame. The connecting structure concentrated around the seat support and front section, with storage behind the seat. Carrying and resting therefore shared the same frame, access space, and clearances.

At checkout, I look at the reach into storage and the space around the tray. For resting, I look at the seat, backrest, and their supports. The drawings and prototype let me inspect these relationships directly. We completed the layout and fabrication study; load capacity and comfort would need separate measurements.
