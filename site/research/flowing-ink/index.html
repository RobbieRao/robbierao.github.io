Flowing Ink Resonator: An Embodied Soundscape for Culturally Grounded Authoring under Rare Records

Flowing Ink Resonator turns literary traces of the Grand Canal into a playable audiovisual environment. Rhythmic bodily action selects and shapes reusable sound events while an ink-wash projection responds in real time.

Corresponding author · Research supervision

Xuefei Zhou, Fenggui Rao*, and Zhiyuan Zhou.

CHI Extended Abstracts 2026

Paper↗Notebook↗

Source material and research scope

Historical sound recordings of the Grand Canal are sparse, while literary descriptions preserve scenes, activities, and sound sources. The project converts those descriptions into a callable audiovisual library. Each sound retains its sentence and source record, so the installation can reuse it without losing the interpretation made during authoring.

My contribution as a corresponding author focuses on the research design and interpretation. The method separates resource authoring from live interaction. Historical interpretation belongs to the selection, annotation, and review of assets; the visitor changes how those assets are selected and modulated. The comparison can then hold the material constant while changing the input modality.

This framing also sets the standard for historical claims. A literary description specifies a scene and a way of hearing it, but rarely supplies enough acoustic information to reconstruct an original recording. I treat semantic correspondence and cultural plausibility as authoring criteria. The research question then concerns how people explore an interpreted soundscape through action, with the source connection available for inspection. This keeps the historical material useful without assigning the generated clip an authenticity that the source cannot establish.

Corpus and reusable sound events

The corpus contains 4,328 filtered text records and 1,120 sentence-level sound descriptions. Annotations identify the scene, sound-source category, temporal form, and affect. Scene-sound tables connect an invoked resource to its prompt, semantic unit, and original record. The event is therefore stored with the information needed to inspect its origin and intended use.

The four annotation dimensions have different jobs. Scene places a sound in an activity or environment; source category identifies what produces it; temporal form distinguishes sustained texture from an event with a beginning and end; affect records the tone of the description. The authoring table preserves these dimensions together. When reviewing a candidate, a plausible source sound is only the first check: its duration, pacing, and relation to the scene also determine whether it can function as a reusable event.

The team generates candidate clips from selected sentences, reviews their semantic match and cultural plausibility, then trims and mixes the accepted material into reusable events. Curated ink seeds provide the visual conditioning inputs. At runtime, the system selects, layers, and shapes these prepared resources. Audio generation remains outside the live control loop, so an action does not require a new sound-generation request before playback can respond.

Sensing, event triggers, and modulation

Projection and multichannel audio establish a virtual boat position. MediaPipe extracts body keypoints and gesture states from the camera stream. Confidence filtering removes unreliable estimates, and temporal smoothing stabilizes the motion features before they become controls. OSC carries those controls between the sensing, sound, and visual components.

The controls have two functions. Action states select or trigger sound events; continuously varying motion features modulate playback and visual parameters. Max/MSP handles the prepared audio clips and mixing. TouchDesigner renders the ink composition through StreamDiffusion conditioned on the active visual seeds. The two engines exchange OSC messages to keep their responses temporally related.

In research guidance, I distinguish event errors from modulation behavior. An unintended trigger changes the selected content; excessive smoothing changes the timing of an otherwise correct continuous response. They affect the experience differently and should remain separate when discussing the mapping or interpreting participant reports.

I use the separation between discrete and continuous control to review the comparison design. Repeating a button press can reveal whether a particular event has been selected. A continuous body movement instead exposes how a parameter evolves over time, including the delay introduced by smoothing. Holding both the event library and modulation space constant gives those observations a common basis. Otherwise, a preference for one condition could be a preference for its sound inventory or visual range rather than its input method.

Pilot study and observed tradeoffs

Six volunteers aged 11 to 75 completed both input conditions, producing twelve sessions. Full-body control and button/keyboard control accessed the same events and the same continuous modulation space. After each session, four seven-point ratings measured control, presence, onboarding effort, and satisfaction; a semi-structured interview captured how the interaction was understood.

The team recorded and transcribed sessions, coded meaning units, grouped codes into themes, and refined them through comparison across conditions. Interview themes are the main results. Paired ratings and Cohen’s dz provide descriptive triangulation; the study did not use hypothesis testing for this small, heterogeneous sample.

Bodily interaction supported continuity: participants related rhythmic effort to changes in the unfolding scene. Symbolic controls supported verification because the same input could be repeated to check its effect. Presence ratings were descriptively higher and less variable with bodily control, while control ratings favored the symbolic condition. Onboarding differed across participants despite similar group means; older adults were sensitive to abrupt changes, and participants with analytic expectations more readily checked input-output rules.

The interviews give the ratings a more precise interpretation. Similar average onboarding scores can conceal very different difficulties: one person may need to discover a control rule, while another may understand the rule but find a sudden sensory transition uncomfortable. I read those accounts against the event/modulation distinction and the two conditions. That is why the analysis stays with themes and paired descriptive estimates rather than treating six participants as enough to establish a general advantage for bodily control.

The design implication is to retain gradual transitions while making the active event easier to identify. A small event cue addresses the uncertainty reported in symbolic-control interviews without requiring the visitor to interrupt the scene. This is a direction derived from the pilot; the pilot itself establishes the contrast between continuity and causal clarity.
