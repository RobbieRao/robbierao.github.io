TrailTracking: AI-Driven Distributed Narratives of Descendant Civilizations in a Digitally Encoded Cosmos

TrailTracking builds a science-fiction game narrative around a civilization discovering its artificial universe. Exploration, archaeology and puzzles turn the setting’s hidden history into something the player can uncover.

My workGame concept and environmental narrative design

Fenggui Rao, Jiaqi Zheng, and Ziqi Wang.

Science 24 Hours, 2024

Paper↗Notebook↗

A hidden history needed places where it could be found

I built the concept around a civilization living inside an artificial universe made by humans, without knowing its origin. That gives the story a secret, but it does not yet give the player a reason to move. I used spaceship travel, human ruins and environmental puzzles to decide where each part of the history could become available.

The project reached the stage of a concept for the world, levels and transitions. I organized that proposal around access: which place could be reached, which object could be read, and what became available afterward. A ruined building establishes a past civilization; an inscription makes that past partly readable; a laboratory offers a more exact account. Those were different ways of revealing the history, rather than interchangeable backdrops.

Crossing the laboratory door changed knowledge and capability

In the proposed opening, piloting comes before departure. Human ruins then introduce interpretation and dark-matter exploration, followed by the AI called Zero. Later segments move toward the center of the universe and the player’s identity, before the proposed escape into reality. I arranged the route so that new story information arrived alongside a change in what the player could do.

The laboratory joins two kinds of progress. Decoding the password gives access to Zero and the dark-matter scanner, so crossing the door changes both knowledge and capability. I place the explanation of the controlled universe after the player has encountered evidence for it. The answer then connects things already seen instead of arriving as an isolated speech.

Putting those transitions in sequence helped me distinguish a piece of lore from a playable premise. Piloting makes departure possible; reading makes a code intelligible; reaching the laboratory provides a tool. The setting became more concrete when I could name those consequences. A longer history of the universe would not, on its own, have explained what happened at the door.

The numeral puzzle exposed a problem with access

I chose seal-script numerals from zero to nine as the password vocabulary. A finite set can be learned and reused, while still looking like a trace of the earlier civilization. The same symbols can be encountered as inscriptions and later entered at a door. Their appearance and function then belong to one reading task.

Reading the sequence back exposed an unresolved dependency in the concept: the numeral posters were inside the laboratory, but the player needed a password to enter. The visual language existed, yet the route had not made its decoding rule available at the point of use. The obstacle was in the order of access, not in the appearance of the numerals.

That small mismatch was more instructive than adding another page of symbols. I had been treating the inscription, poster and lock as related visual elements; their locations also determined whether the puzzle could be understood at all. The concept made their shared style visible, but it did not yet resolve that spatial dependency. I came away reading a puzzle’s layout as part of its logic.

From distant navigation to reading an inscription

I gave the environments different viewing distances in the concept. The bright, open cosmos works at navigation scale; damaged ruins and old Chinese inscriptions invite closer inspection. Blue light, equipment and a luminous interface concentrate the laboratory around tools and the encounter with the controlling system. The changes in visual scale followed what the player was meant to examine at each stop.

The ship transition draws on Kun/Peng imagery. In the proposed transformation, propulsion parts are discarded, leaving a simpler blue-white core cabin for the wormhole passage; surrounding scenery moves rapidly backward during the jump. I paired the change in the vehicle with the change in the outside view, giving the transition two visual cues instead of leaving its meaning to explanatory text.

Working through the route changed where I looked for detail. The large-scale world gave the journey its atmosphere, but a numeral on a wall, a door and a newly available tool determined how its history could be discovered. This remained a concept rather than a tested game. Its most useful part for me was the connection between those scales: the world had to be readable through the objects and transitions inside it.
