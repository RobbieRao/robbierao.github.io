TrailTracking: AI-Driven Distributed Narratives of Descendant Civilizations in a Digitally Encoded Cosmos

TrailTracking builds a science-fiction game narrative around a civilization discovering its artificial universe. Exploration, archaeology and puzzles turn the setting’s hidden history into something the player can uncover.

Fenggui Rao, Jiaqi Zheng, and Ziqi Wang.

Science 24 Hours, 2024

Paper↗Notebook↗

A world revealed through exploration

I developed the game concept around a descendant civilization living inside an artificial universe created by humans. Its inhabitants do not know where their world came from. Spaceship exploration, investigation of human ruins, and environmental puzzles are the proposed ways to uncover that history. The design problem is to make information become available through places and actions, with each discovery changing what the player can pursue.

The project remains a concept design, with a proposed world, level sequence, environments, and ship transitions. I organize the notes around access: which place the player can reach, which object they can interpret, and which piece of history becomes intelligible there. This gives the narrative a spatial structure that can later be translated into playable encounters.

Human ruins are especially useful because they can carry several levels of evidence at once. Their damaged architecture establishes a lost civilization; inscriptions suggest that the remains can be read; the laboratory promises a more specific explanation. The sequence needs to make these clues increasingly actionable, so that reaching the laboratory feels like the result of learning how to read the world.

Learning to travel, then learning to read

The opening teaches piloting before departure. The next segment takes the player into human ruins, introduces dark-matter exploration, and brings an encounter with the AI called Zero. The route then moves toward the universe’s center and the player’s identity, before a proposed escape from the artificial universe into reality. Each segment adds a new question while using a capability introduced earlier.

The laboratory joins progression and disclosure. Its password gate requires the player to interpret the surrounding material; inside are Zero and the dark-matter scanner. Entry therefore changes both the information available and the tools available. The AI’s explanation of the controlled universe needs to arrive after the player has already encountered traces that make the revelation meaningful.

I use this sequence to check what each encounter contributes. Piloting establishes movement; ruins introduce interpretation; the password requires a rule to be applied; the laboratory delivers a tool and a narrative answer. If several stages only add exposition, the route loses its playable structure. The useful detail is the action required at each boundary and what becomes possible immediately afterward.

Numerals, evidence, and a locked boundary

The password vocabulary uses seal-script numerals from zero to nine. Keeping the set finite gives the puzzle a rule that can be learned and reused. The same writing can appear as a trace of the earlier civilization and as an input at the laboratory door. Its visual character and its function in the puzzle then support the same act of reading.

Clue placement needs an explicit access check. The current concept puts the numeral posters inside the laboratory while also requiring a password to enter it. The player needs an earlier way to see or infer the decoding rule. Three possible layouts are to move the clue into the preceding ruins, allow it to be read through an opening, or make the password a second lock inside an initially accessible laboratory. The drawing below makes that dependency visible.

Each alternative produces a different encounter. A clue in the ruins rewards attentive exploration; an external view asks the player to inspect the architecture; an internal second lock lets the environment teach the notation before testing it. I would choose among them according to the intended action, then specify what the player sees after a correct or incorrect input. Adding more symbols before resolving that sequence would increase visual material without clarifying the puzzle.

Environment and vehicle transitions

The environment distinguishes an expansive, bright cosmos from damaged human ruins carrying old Chinese inscriptions. The laboratory introduces blue light, equipment, and a luminous interface. These spaces offer different reading distances: the cosmos supports navigation, the ruins invite close inspection, and the laboratory concentrates attention around tools and an encounter with the system controlling the universe.

The ship transformation draws on Kun/Peng imagery. After propulsion components are discarded, a simpler blue-white core cabin remains for the wormhole passage. The proposed jump sends surrounding scenery rapidly backward. The transition has two readable parts: the vehicle changes configuration, and the visual field changes around it. The player can recognize a shift in travel state through both the object they occupy and the space outside it.

The next useful prototype is a short route from ruins to laboratory. It would combine symbol recognition, access to the clue, password entry, and the reveal of Zero or the scanner. That segment is small enough to inspect the order of evidence and feedback before building the larger journey. The broader world already supplies a destination; this slice would establish how the player earns the next discovery.
