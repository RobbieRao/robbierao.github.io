RESEARCH / 2026

Crowdsourcing StreetCure: A Workshop-Derived Human-AI Pipeline for Restorative Street Micro-Interventions

StreetCure connects residents’ street experiences to measurable variables and generative redesign. Its three-stage workflow grew out of a Guangzhou workshop and was formalized into a mobile-first prototype.

Unity · VR/MR prototype development

Yuhao He, Tiantian Lo, Yongming Chen, and Fenggui Rao.

eCAADe 2026

Paper↗Notebook↗

From interviews to assessment categories

The workshop preceded the formal StreetCure pipeline. Its tasks and resulting materials were reorganized into Crowd-Define, Crowd-Measure, and Crowd-Remix. At the workshop, the mobile app was only partially deployed; some actions used prepared media and interface proxies. This matters for development because the integrated workflow describes connections that the pilot tested through more than one medium.

Crowd-Define used AI-guided interviews with ten residents, each lasting about an hour. Residents watched designated street videos and described restorative and non-restorative impressions. The team embedded the transcripts with all-MiniLM-L12-v2, reduced them to 12 dimensions with UMAP, and applied HDBSCAN. The environmental-variable analysis produced 22 clusters, with noise 0.10 and silhouette 0.563.

The LLM summarized excerpts and linked clusters; researchers made the final coding decisions. The result was five environmental themes and thirteen sub-themes, plus a separate synthesis of perceptual mechanisms. For the prototype, these categories specify what the assessment interface needs to ask. Keeping the underlying statement accessible preserves the difference between a resident’s description and the team’s category label.

Image-level measurements

Ten residents assessed fifty street photographs. Each image received two independent ratings, whose mean became the image-level value used in analysis. Residents rated environmental sub-themes, overall restorativeness, perceived comfort, and visual pleasure. GPS metadata linked each entry to a location.

Mask2Former supplied semantic segmentation for greenery, water, and visible-sky ratios. Variables requiring interpretation remained human-rated. Normalized sub-variable scores were aggregated into theme-level scores. This combination preserves a distinction between image composition, such as the fraction occupied by greenery, and a perceptual response such as comfort.

My Unity/VR work in this research line centers on scene presentation and participant input. A response needs an identifiable scene and question before it can become an analysis record. Changing the displayed scene, changing an answer, and advancing to the next item are separate actions; the client must keep the response associated with the material actually assessed.

For a segmented image, a greenery ratio is the number of pixels assigned to greenery divided by the image area; water and visible sky are computed in the same way. These values describe the photograph’s composition. A camera direction that excludes the sky changes the sky ratio even if the street itself is unchanged. The photograph must therefore stay attached to the measurement so that the frame being evaluated remains inspectable.

The two resident scores are averaged within each photograph before model fitting. This produces fifty image-level observations, rather than treating the two responses as independent copies of the same visual input. The individual scores still contain information about disagreement that the mean removes. For the interaction record, preserving who responded to which image and question supports that distinction without changing the analysis unit.

Prepared alternatives and resident feedback

For Crowd-Remix, the team generated fifty alternatives with Gemini 2.5 Flash Image. The targets came from the preceding assessment, including limited greenery, weak visual order, and insufficient resting facilities. Preparing the images reduced on-site generation time. Ten residents reviewed them, each providing ten likes and ten comments.

Prepared material lets the pilot examine reactions to a known comparison set. A live generation client adds another set of requirements: pending requests, results arriving after a participant changes view, and regeneration. These behaviors need implementation beyond replacing a stored image with an API response. The workshop’s preference results describe the proposals people saw, not the latency or reliability of that future live path.

Higher-liked variants commonly improved spatial order, façade color, seating, small-scale greenery, and surface treatment while retaining everyday street logic. More intensive transformation was not consistently preferred. The comments supplied reasons for these preferences, so they remained associated with the relevant proposal alongside the quicker like signal.

Model comparison and design implications

The team fitted multiple linear regression and a random forest to the image-level data. The linear model yielded R² = 0.770 on the fitted pilot data and predictor VIFs below 5. It also outperformed the random forest under cross-validation. The forest remained an exploratory comparison; its SHAP analysis ranked aesthetic factors most strongly, followed by spatial, natural, and multisensory themes.

Mediation analysis used OLS with 5,000 bootstrap resamples. In the joint model, comfort carried indirect effects for spatial, multisensory, aesthetic, and social domains; visual pleasure retained an independent indirect effect only for the aesthetic domain. The analysis therefore gives more specific design targets than a single instruction to add greenery. A proposal can address comfort through arrangement and maintenance, or visual pleasure through façade and surface changes.

The sample contains fifty photographs from one city. Researcher-selected media and ontology coding also shaped which variables entered the models. The fitted R² describes this pilot; the cross-validation comparison is more relevant to choosing between its two models. A deployed client would still need participant-led capture and prompting to examine how the workflow changes outside the prepared workshop material.

The cross-validation result is relevant to model selection because both models were fitted to a small image-level dataset. A more flexible model can explain variation in its training images without providing the more stable held-out prediction. Keeping the linear model as the stronger predictive account and the forest as an exploratory comparison gives their outputs different roles: the regression summarizes associations, while SHAP helps inspect how the forest uses its inputs.
