RESEARCH / 2026

Streets as Shared Canvases: An In-Situ Mixed Reality Platform for Citizen-Led Urban Restorative Design

An in-situ mixed-reality platform brings street assessment and design into the same walk. People capture their surroundings, describe their experience and develop scene-conditioned alternatives with AI and other participants.

Unity · VR/MR prototype development

Yuhao He, Tian Tian Lo, and Fenggui Rao.

CAADRIA 2026

Paper↗Notebook↗

Capture and Explore use different references

Capture Mode starts from the street in front of the participant. Explore Mode starts from a proposal made by somebody else. Both collect spoken impressions and desired improvements, followed by a brief 1–7 restorativeness rating. The shared input format keeps the walking interaction short; the different reference determines whether the result begins a new sequence or extends an existing one.

In the Unity interface, the reference image is part of the task context. An Explore request inherits a previous visual change. A chronological feed alone cannot express that relationship, because a newly created proposal may derive from an older image rather than the most recent item. The research record therefore uses explicit links between a proposal and the image selected as its reference.

The restorativeness input is a single overall item instead of a multi-item questionnaire at every stop. This reduces interruption during on-foot use, at the cost of a less detailed appraisal for each scene. SEQ and SUS are collected after the walking tasks to evaluate operation of the prototype separately.

In the illustrated lineage, the first proposal adds shade to a captured scene. Explore then uses that proposal to request seating, so the second edit inherits the shade as part of its input. Starting the same seating request from the original capture would form another branch. A chronological list can place both results next to each other, but only the parent reference explains why their starting conditions differ.

Scene-conditioned generation and screening

The LLM interprets the spoken request into a semantic brief. NanoBanana generates a visual update conditioned on the reference scene. Generation completed within ten seconds in the field deployment. Returned images were then screened for semantic consistency, scene fidelity, and plausible scale; failed cases were regenerated with tighter prompts or logged and excluded.

Scale distortion affects the proposal even when the image loads correctly. Planting or street furniture must fit the scene’s proportions, and a bench must leave a usable walking route. Screening therefore changes the client workflow: delivery of an image and availability of an accepted proposal are separate stages. An excluded output should not become the reference for another participant’s Explore task.

NanoBanana is used as an off-the-shelf generator. The evaluated workflow includes screening and retry. For my development work, waiting, result presentation, and acceptance belong to the same task; measuring response time only at the network return would omit part of the operation.

Event records and the provenance graph

The field study covered a 400-meter section of Chatham Road South. Twenty participants were randomly assigned to one of two pedestrian sides: a conventional sidewalk with commercial and residential frontage, or a corridor containing micro-plazas. Each completed a twenty-minute walk with approximately ten Capture or Explore tasks.

The platform recorded coordinates, restorativeness scores, verbal feedback, and interaction events. The pilot produced 196 valid co-creation events. In the provenance graph, each node is a captured or generated image; a directed link records which earlier image was used as the reference. The same original scene can support several branches of edits.

Those links support a different analysis from the spatial map. Coordinates locate an encounter; reference links reconstruct derivation; verbal feedback supplies the participant’s reason for a change. Longer branches indicate repeated attention to a scene. They need to be read with the associated comments and ratings to understand what participants were trying to improve.

An image reference is also different from a place reference. Several captures can concern the same stretch of street without being revisions of one another. Conversely, a sequence of generated proposals can inherit one capture while accumulating edits. Grouping by coordinates answers where activity occurred; grouping by reference links answers how a proposal developed. The scene image and event record let those two views be checked against one another.

Field results and prototype constraints

The co-creation phase received a mean SEQ score of 6.0. Mean SUS was 73.9, with Cronbach’s α = 0.867. These results were obtained with Meta Quest 3, which substituted for the lightweight MR glasses envisioned by the design. The headset choice matters when interpreting comfort and how much attention the interface demands during a walk.

After co-creation, 70.62% of samples received a higher perceived-restorativeness rating, 19.07% were unchanged, and 10.31% decreased. These are ratings of visual proposals in the pilot. The study did not construct the interventions or measure their subsequent effects on the street.

The team’s analysis of spoken accounts used MiniLM-L12-v2 embeddings, UMAP reduction to 20 dimensions, and HDBSCAN. The selected setting produced 29 clusters with 0.30 noise, DBCV 0.359, and silhouette 0.583; researcher interpretation consolidated them into five themes and thirteen sub-themes. This analysis depends on the client retaining the verbal account with its scene, instead of storing only the final image.

Resting amenities and micro-greenery appeared as frequently noticed, positively appraised themes. Maintenance and façade discord were also frequently mentioned but associated with lower ratings. Mention frequency therefore cannot substitute for preference: the record needs the appraisal and the account of what was noticed.
