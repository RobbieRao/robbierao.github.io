CityCure: Walk, Capture, and Reimagine Street Micro-Interventions with In-Situ MR and GenAI

CityCure makes a street-design proposal part of an ordinary walk. Gesture capture, spoken intentions and mixed-reality alternatives connect a noticed problem to a proposal that others can discuss.

Unity · VR/MR prototype development

Yuhao He, Fenggui Rao, and Tian Tian Lo.

CHI Extended Abstracts 2026

Paper↗Notebook↗

Scene capture and spoken input

The interaction starts during a walk. A capture gesture records the street scene; spoken input describes the desired change; a seven-point street-quality rating records the participant’s appraisal. These inputs have separate roles. The image supplies spatial context, the request specifies an intervention, and the score records an evaluation of the existing place.

The storyboard’s example asks for shade and a bench while keeping the sidewalk clear. The constraint belongs with the requested objects: a bench placed across the walking route would satisfy the object description but conflict with the request. Keeping the surrounding scene visible gives the participant a reference for checking the generated proposal.

My development focus is the transition from this input into the immersive comparison interface. The scene being discussed must remain identifiable when alternatives appear. Otherwise, the participant has to reconstruct the original request from memory while judging a changed image.

Region selection and proposal refinement

An optional bounding box localizes the intervention within the captured image. It reduces the burden of describing a position verbally while preserving the rest of the street as context. The selected region and the source image therefore need different treatment: one identifies the intended change area, while the other remains the comparison reference.

The workflow presents three alternatives, then allows selection and refinement. Refinement starts from a specific candidate, so selecting another image changes the material being edited. This distinction affects the client’s state: the source scene, active candidate, and subsequent request must remain associated through the transition.

The visible checks are spatially specific: whether the intervention occupies the selected area, whether the surrounding street remains recognizable, and whether pedestrian clearance is retained. They let a person assess the result against the original request before sharing it.

The two views in the spatial sketch retain the same street geometry. The box expresses the intervention’s location; the open corridor supplies a separate constraint. This separation is useful when comparing candidates: a result can satisfy the requested object list while altering the surrounding scene or narrowing access. The comparison therefore needs both the selected region and the untouched context, rather than a crop of the proposed bench alone.

Reading the five-step interaction

StepInformation carried forward
Walk & noticeA situated concern about one place.
CaptureA visual reference for that concern.
VoiceDesired change plus street-quality appraisal.
Generate & chooseAlternative proposals and a selected direction.
Share & discussA proposal card with collective constraints and comments.

Information retained between steps

Capture establishes the source scene. Expression adds the spoken request and appraisal. Comparison introduces alternative images, and selection identifies the one used for refinement. The final card carries that selected proposal into discussion. This sequence defines the information needed at each transition in the Unity client.

The source scene has a longer lifetime than an individual comparison view. It is still needed after a candidate is selected, because later changes must be judged against the original place. A screen refresh and a new selection also have different meanings: refreshing presentation should not silently replace the proposal under discussion.

For refinement, I distinguish the original capture from the candidate currently under edit. The original answers what the street looked like; the selected candidate answers which changes have already been accepted for this round. A further request must refer to that candidate. Returning to comparison or selecting another candidate changes the edit reference, while the original capture remains available. Those are separate transitions in the interaction even when the same panel displays the images.

The proposal card is the point where those associations have to survive outside the editing view. Someone reading a comment about access needs to identify the exact proposal, not whichever alternative is currently visible in the author’s session. This is why selection state belongs with the proposal being shared, while temporary presentation state belongs to the client view.

Proposal cards and community feedback

The final step posts a proposal card to a community feed. The illustrated comments address emergency access, shade, feasibility, and regulations. These concerns extend the initial request; they cannot be resolved by inspecting the generated image’s appearance alone. The card gives other residents a concrete proposal to respond to.

For the client, sharing is a handoff from an individual session to people who did not make the capture. The selected proposal needs its scene and request beside it so that comments refer to an identifiable change. This is the endpoint I use when checking the interaction: the information collected at the beginning still has to explain the proposal after it leaves the headset.
