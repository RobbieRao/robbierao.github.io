RESEARCH / 2025

The Immersive Art Therapy Driven by AIGC: An Innovative Approach to Alleviating Children's Nyctophobia

An immersive drawing installation explores how children can create reassuring imagery in a darkened space. Its design connects a child-art LoRA, tangible tracking and multi-surface projection.

Corresponding author · Research supervision

Jinlin Miao, Zhiyuan Zhou, Yilei Wu, Fenggui Rao*, and Fanjing Meng.

CHI Extended Abstracts 2025

Project↗Paper↗Code↗Notebook↗

Interviews and design requirements

The team conducted ten semi-structured interviews with children, family members, and specialists. Mandarin interview records were coded independently, then discussed to form themes. These included fear associated with darkness and enclosed spaces, reassurance from light and companionship, and the role of creative activity. The design translates them into surrounding projection, stylized fear-related imagery, and movement that changes the scene.

In research supervision, I connect each requirement to the interaction being evaluated. Creative expression requires an action with a visible consequence. The surrounding projection needs to preserve that relationship across the room. The interview findings about companionship also affect the arrangement of participants: the child’s activity takes place with possible support from a parent or facilitator.

The interview-to-design connection is quite concrete. Reassurance from light makes lighting intensity and transitions part of the interaction; fear of enclosed darkness makes the room arrangement relevant; companionship makes the parent or facilitator part of the use situation. As a corresponding author, I focus on whether the prototype and evaluation retain those relationships. A visually attractive projection can still miss the design requirement if the participant cannot influence its intensity or understand the consequence of a movement.

Visual style and spatial interaction

The imagery uses a LoRA trained on 423 drawings from children aged 7 to 12. The team cropped the training images, extracted content and style prompts with DeepBooru, and trained the LoRA with Kohya’s tools. This preparation gives the generated assets a drawing style related to the intended participants.

The preparation of the drawing dataset connects content labels with style adaptation. Cropping makes the training images consistent enough to process together, while DeepBooru-derived tags describe the depicted content and accompany the images as text. Kohya trains the LoRA on those image-text pairs. The 423 drawings are a style-training resource; they are not a sample of children who received the intervention. I keep the visual-model preparation separate from the participant and specialist evidence when framing the contribution.

Stylized characters and flowing particles respond to physical movement across the projected environment. A marker identifies both an input position and the prompt associated with that object. The installation therefore connects the child’s movement to the selection and placement of visual material. The two walls and floor extend this feedback around the participant.

The design review needs to distinguish visual activity from understandable control. A child must be able to locate the response to their own action within the larger scene. Increasing particle density or adding another character changes the composition, while the action-response mapping remains a separate interaction decision.

Local tracking and image generation

The local Python program detects ArUco identities and coordinates. It sends prompt and trajectory data to the generation service, while a UDP stream sends coordinates directly to TouchDesigner. These paths have different update schedules: movement updates the current scene continuously, and image generation returns a new asset after processing.

The marker carries two pieces of information that should remain distinguishable in the implementation: its identity selects associated prompt content, while its coordinates describe movement. The local program packages trajectory information as JSON for the generation service; the direct UDP path keeps positional feedback available in the current composition. Treating every coordinate change as a reason to wait for a newly generated image would couple bodily feedback to a much slower operation. The split paths allow the composition to keep moving while the asset changes on its own schedule.

OpenCV GrabCut separates the returned image foreground before TouchDesigner composites it with the scene. Spout streams the output into Resolume, which slices and transforms the image for two vertical walls and the floor. A marker-detection problem, an incomplete foreground mask, and an output-mapping problem can therefore produce different faults at the projection surfaces.

I use these component boundaries to make technical feedback specific: check the detected coordinates, the returned asset and mask, then the spatial output. Tracking responsiveness and asset-generation time need separate discussion because they affect different parts of the interaction.

The final composition also has a spatial boundary. A correct image on the workstation does not yet establish a correct image on the floor and walls. GrabCut determines which pixels belong to the foreground; TouchDesigner determines their composition and particle behavior; Resolume determines the slice and transform sent to each surface. I organize technical review around those distinct outputs so that a masking issue is not mistaken for a projection-calibration issue, and a correct calibration is not taken as evidence that the interaction is understandable.

Specialist review and evaluation scope

Three psychology experts and one art-therapy expert reviewed diagrams and a demonstration video. Their feedback concerned gradual exposure, differences between children, and parental involvement. This places control over pace and intensity within the design requirements, alongside the visual content.

The specialist feedback also changes the order of evaluation. Before discussing longer-term outcomes, the next interaction study needs to establish whether the child can recognize their own input, whether the accompanying adult can intervene, and whether changes in visual intensity remain tolerable. Those observations require the system state and the operator of each control to be clear. They would turn “adaptable experience” into specific behaviors that can be observed during a session.

For the next prototype, lowering intensity, pausing, and returning to familiar feedback are candidate controls. Each requires a clear operator: the child, a parent, or the facilitator. A study of these controls would examine whether participants understand and use the choices. The completed specialist review evaluates the design direction; it does not establish a therapeutic outcome over time.
