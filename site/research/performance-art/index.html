Enlivening Performance Art: Enhanced Interactivity through Embodied Cognition and Real-Time Physical Visualization on Swarm Tangible Interfaces

A swarm tangible interface connects physical robots, tracking and projected visuals for live performance. The prototype extends Rovable-style fabric climbing into a spatial installation that performers and audiences can manipulate.

Circuit and PCB design

I was the third author of this project and independently designed the circuit schematic and PCB for the adapted robotic unit. The starting point was Rovable: small robots use magnetic wheels to travel on clothing. Our team extended that idea to larger fabric surfaces for spatial performance. My hardware work provided the control, programming, charging, and motor interfaces needed by the revised unit.

The controller uses an ESP32-C3FH4 and its Wi-Fi capability to control the wheels. This decision places wireless commands, local motor outputs, and a practical development interface on the same board. It also makes the circuit part of the installation’s working process: a unit has to be programmable and serviceable after it has been assembled into the mechanical structure.

The circuit sits inside a mechanical system: wheel geometry and magnetic mounting constrain component placement, connector access, and the loads served by the two motor outputs. The wheel and mounting work was part of the team’s wider adaptation. My focus was to make the electrical interfaces clear within that assembly.

For an installation, those interfaces matter as much as the processor choice. A board that can receive a wireless command still needs a usable path for charging, firmware updates, motor connection, and recovery. I read the design through those operations first; the component list follows from them.

Reading the power and control paths

The schematic has a USB Type-C input, a TP4056 charging circuit, a battery connector, and an NCP1117-3.3 regulator. The TP4056 exposes charging and standby indications through CHRG and STDBY. The regulator produces the +3.3 V net used by the ESP32-C3FH4, CH340C, and both motor-driver sections. Reading the charging path separately from the regulated supply makes the source of each rail and the role of the battery connector easier to follow.

USB also provides a data path. Its D+ and D− signals connect to the CH340C USB-to-serial interface, whose transmit and receive signals connect to the controller. Two S8050 transistors form the download-control logic; the schematic also includes a hardware reset button. These are the connections that make firmware loading and recovery possible without relying on the wireless application already running correctly.

The two motor channels are explicit. Each TC118S receives its own pair of control inputs—INA1/INB1 or INA2/INB2—and exposes OUTA/OUTB to a two-pin motor connector. The schematic includes capacitors around the driver outputs and local supply sections. Separating the channels in the drawing helps trace a command from a controller pin to the appropriate motor connector and keeps the two sides of the wheel assembly identifiable during integration.

The ESP32 section includes the antenna connection, clock circuitry, supply connections, and reset-related nets around the controller. Together with the serial and motor sections, it gives the board three distinct responsibilities: maintain a programmable controller, accept remote control, and deliver local actuation. PCB decisions and system interfaces

My PCB work translates those electrical relationships into a board that fits the robotic unit. I treat power routing, signal routing, radio placement, and access for assembly as separate checks. The motor connections carry a changing load; the controller, serial interface, and wireless section need a stable supply and usable signal paths. Supply and return paths therefore deserve attention before filling the remaining area with convenient-looking traces.

For the layout review, the schematic supplies a concrete checklist: the regulator and its capacitors, the charging circuit and battery connector, USB data and the CH340C, the download/reset network, and the two motor-output sections. Local supply capacitors should remain close to the circuits they serve; motor current paths and their return paths need deliberate placement. The antenna connection adds another spatial constraint, while the Type-C port, reset button, battery, and motor connectors must remain accessible in the assembled structure.

The mechanical work and electrical work meet at the wheels. The team compared tread structures and found stronger fabric adhesion with more tread fold inflection points in the tested variants. That result concerns the wheel geometry and fabric contact. On the electrical side, I keep the two motor channels and their connection points explicit so that changes to the mechanical assembly do not obscure which output drives which wheel.

The rest of the installation adds a tracking and visual layer around the robotic units. Magnetically mounted ArUco markers are observed by IP cameras, and OpenCV estimates position and orientation. Those estimates feed procedural patterns and StreamDiffusion-based image transformations. These were team-system interfaces around my hardware contribution. A motor command, an estimated pose, and a projected pattern are three different signals, each with a different place to inspect when the installation behaves unexpectedly.

A practical bring-up sequence

The board gives a natural order for bring-up: check the supply rails and connections, establish USB serial communication, verify download and reset, then exercise one motor channel at a time before introducing Wi-Fi control. This is the verification sequence I would use for a new board revision. It keeps a problem in power, programming, or a particular output channel from being hidden inside the complete installation.

System integration adds another sequence: command a physical movement, confirm that the marker tracking reports the corresponding pose change, and then check the visual mapping. A projection may continue to animate even when the pose estimate has stopped changing; a wheel can receive a command without moving as expected against the fabric. Looking at these signals separately is useful when designing a rehearsal setup and deciding what status information needs to remain visible.

The team demonstrated the integrated prototype in a live exhibition. It established a working combination of fabric movement, tracked spatial data, and responsive imagery. The remaining work includes more efficient cluster control and further development of the robots’ autonomy. A larger group of units would make addressing, connection state, recovery, and coordinated commands more prominent engineering concerns than they are in a single-unit check.

For the next hardware revision, I would first improve the information available during those checks: accessible rail measurements, clearly identified motor connectors, and an unambiguous programming and recovery path. These changes should be judged in the assembled robot, where the fabric, magnets, and enclosure determine what can actually be reached. That is where a circuit drawing becomes maintainable installation hardware.
