DisFace3DNet: Explainable Facial Attractiveness Prediction via 3D Component Disentanglement

DisFace3DNet turns an overall facial-attractiveness prediction into seven component scores with explicit numerical contributions. The research connects controllable 3D representations with a prediction that can be inspected part by part.

Fenggui Rao, Yan Luximon*, and Jie Zhang*.

arXiv:2610.11656 · Preprint

Project↗Paper↗Code (soon)↗Notebook↗

Research objective and evaluation protocol

I wanted to distinguish facial shape, skin, illumination, hair, background, expression, and pose within an attractiveness predictor. Grad-CAM localizes image regions associated with an output, but several factors can occupy the same region. A cheek carries shape, skin, and lighting information simultaneously. I therefore separated the input representations before learning the scores.

SCUT-FBP5500 provides overall ratings for 5,500 images on a scale of 1 to 5, without independently measured component ratings. The branches learn jointly from the overall rating, with auxiliary weak labels guiding their interpretation. Five scores in [0, 1] enter the static term through positive normalized weights, followed by a bias; expression and pose provide signed adjustments.

We used the publisher's fixed five folds: 4,400 training and 1,100 test images per fold, with a 3,960/440 inner-training/validation split. Inner-validation PCC selects the checkpoint. At fusion, the static/dynamic total-score statistics and coefficients use inner-training data only and are fixed before test evaluation. Matched controls share the folds and paired seeds.

Constructing the seven component inputs

The preprocessing combines FFHQ-UV, Deep3DFaceRecon, and HiFi3D++ for neutral geometry, albedo, and illumination; 3DDFA-V3 for expression and pose; and BiSeNet for hair and background segmentation. Image and UV inputs are cached at 384 × 384 before attractiveness training. Reusing this cache keeps preprocessing fixed across folds and matched controls.

Identity receives positions, normals, and curvature, plus three symmetry and four scale descriptors. Skin receives albedo and texture detail without shape coordinates. Lighting evaluates spherical harmonics on neutral-mesh normals. This representation choice limits the information available to each scorer before the loss functions constrain its output.

The channel counts make the separation concrete. Identity uses nine UV channels: positions, normals, and three curvature quantities. Skin uses twelve: RGB albedo and RGB detail at fine, middle, and coarse scales. Illumination uses three UV channels reconstructed from 27 spherical-harmonic coefficients on neutral-mesh normals. Expression uses three displacement-normal channels, three compressed displacement channels, and one log-magnitude channel. The non-RGB inputs require adapted encoder stems; their semantics are fixed by preprocessing rather than learned by choosing arbitrary image regions.

Expression is constructed by subtracting neutral from expressive BFM meshes reconstructed with identical identity coefficients, then transferring the displacement to the HiFi3D++ topology and UV space. Seven channels contain displacement normals, compressed displacement, and log magnitude. Compression uses 3 tanh(z(ΔU)/3); invalid pixels are zeroed. Displacement statistics come from a fixed cache of 500 samples shared across folds.

Pose is a three-dimensional pitch/yaw/roll vector in radians. Hair retains segmented RGB pixels and zeros the rest. Background uses 15 color, gradient, area, and skin-contrast statistics. EfficientNet-B0 processes the UV maps and hair image; MLPs process pose and background. Each branch also receives a shared eight-dimensional embedding of the supplied benchmark-group label through its own FiLM scale and offset. That label is external metadata and remains required at inference.

Joint learning and frozen fusion

We trained in PyTorch with AdamW and batch size 12. The schedule has 70 epochs at 3 × 10⁻⁴ followed by 30 refinement epochs at 3 × 10⁻⁵; both stages jointly optimize all seven routes. EfficientNet-B0 starts from ImageNet weights, with part of the pretrained feature blocks kept frozen. Refinement disables augmentation. Inference averages original and horizontally flipped inputs, with corresponding geometry, displacement, and yaw components reversed.

The objective combines overall-rating error, FPEM distillation, label-distribution learning, scalar/distribution agreement, weak semantic supervision, static-score balance, and pairwise ordering. The distribution head has 21 bins. GPT-5.5 jointly generates the seven weak labels: static targets map to [0, 1], expression to [−0.10, 0.10], and pose to [−0.05, 0.05]. Their masked absolute error is averaged by component with λsem = 0.2. These labels provide auxiliary guidance rather than human component ground truth.

The auxiliary targets constrain different behavior. Label-distribution learning represents the spread of overall ratings across 21 bins from 1 to 5; it supplies information that a mean score discards. The scalar/distribution agreement term uses a stopped gradient on the distribution expectation, so its agreement update acts on the component-score path. Pairwise ordering uses sufficiently separated rating pairs rather than treating nearly tied images as equally informative. These losses complement the weak component labels without turning them into independent measurements of facial factors.

I checked score dispersion separately from prediction accuracy. A static branch can become nearly constant while the other branches still fit the overall rating. The balance term penalizes differences among the log standard deviations of the five static scores. Removing it raised the static-score scale coefficient of variation from 0.082 to 0.377, while PCC changed only slightly.

After checkpoint selection, the entire network is frozen. We standardize the static and dynamic totals with inner-training statistics, then fit two nonnegative fusion weights and an unconstrained intercept by convex optimization. The objective combines squared error to benchmark ratings and 0.3 times squared error to FPEM targets. The teacher is fine-tuned within the same outer fold and selected by inner validation. Only fusion coefficients are updated. FPEM, weak labels, and the distribution head are used during training; FiLM conditioning remains active at deployment.

Expanding the fitted expression produces seven contributions and an effective intercept whose sum reconstructs the normalized prediction before clipping. Each contribution multiplied by four is in rating points; the final rating is clip(1 + 4y, 1, 5). I use the pre-clipping value when checking the decomposition because clipping changes the reported rating at the endpoints without changing the underlying contributions.

Matched controls and human judgments

Five-fold PCC is 0.8904 ± 0.0063; Table I reports MAE 0.258 and RMSE 0.337. FPEM predicts overall ratings more accurately. To isolate the cost of component scoring, we replaced the seven readouts and additive fusion with one scalar head, retaining the inputs, encoders, conditioning, and schedule. This control has only 224 fewer parameters. The additive model's mean MAE is higher by 0.0108 rating points (95% CI 0.0078 to 0.0138).

Component diagnostics reveal changes that PCC does not capture. Removing weak semantic supervision changes expression's weak-label correlation from 0.116 to −0.188, and pose's from 0.212 to −0.022. Compute-matched staged training narrows expression-score SD from 0.0190 to 0.0023 and reduces the fitted dynamic coefficient from 0.0242 to 0.0034. The dynamic branches remain present, but contribute much less variation to the final rating.

These results support retaining joint learning. I also examined semantic-weight sensitivity. Increasing λsem from 0 to 0.2 improved weak-label correspondence for all seven components in all five validation seeds. Larger weights improved some components further, but reduced overall PCC and did not consistently benefit lighting. We fixed 0.2 using validation prediction and component diagnostics, without using the test fold.

Twenty adults each completed 103 human-evaluation trials, including practice, component comparisons, repeats, separate overall comparisons, and attention checks. The component task asks about shape, skin, hair, or expression and allows an unsure response. Model comparison directions use held-out component scores standardized within each official fold. Across the first three components, the model agrees with 676 of 960 decisive responses: 70.4% (participant-clustered 95% CI 67.1% to 73.7%). Expression agreement is 123/294, about 41.8%.

The human-study denominator is part of interpreting the result. The primary agreement analysis excludes unsure responses, which leaves 960 decisive responses for identity, skin, and hair. Counting unsure responses as nonagreement changes the pooled figure to 676/1,200, or 56.3%. Reporting the task and response rule beside the headline number makes clear what 70.4% describes: agreement when a participant made a directional choice, rather than successful explanation of every presented pair.

The expression task asks which expression increases that person's attractiveness more relative to neutral. Participants see only the current photographs and must imagine a neutral reference; between-human agreement is also low. My next experiment would present controlled expressions of the same identity with an observed neutral reference, to distinguish task difficulty from a mismatch in the model score. Lighting, pose, and background were not included in the formal human evaluation.

The additive reconstruction verifies how this model computes a rating. The human comparison tests selected score directions under a specified judgment task. Estimating the perceptual effect of changing a component requires intervention data, particularly for expression.
