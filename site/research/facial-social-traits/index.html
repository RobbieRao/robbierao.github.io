Facial Social Trait Perception, Interpretation, and Prediction: A State-of-the-Art Review

A review organized around the full path from facial variables to human judgments, interpretation and computational prediction. It brings psychology, measurement and machine learning into one research framework.

Fenggui Rao, Yuhao He, Yan Luximon*, and Jie Zhang*.

IEEE Transactions on Affective Computing · Accepted / In press

Paper↗Notebook↗

Organizing the review

I organized the review around perception, interpretation, and prediction. The literature spans psychology, neuroscience, and machine learning, and the same trait name can refer to different experimental targets. A geometric measurement, a participant rating, and a model trained on averaged ratings require separate definitions. Figure 1 connects these stages while retaining the stimulus and measurement procedure behind the prediction target.

I also separate three kinds of model interpretation. Gradient maps show where a trained predictor is sensitive. Occlusion and other perturbations test how its output changes when selected information is removed. Generative editing changes a facial attribute and measures the resulting prediction. Each operation answers a different question: a highlighted eye region does not identify whether shape, texture, or illumination caused the response; an edited image must preserve identity and image fidelity if the change is to be attributed to the intended feature. These distinctions give the interpretation section a usable structure for choosing an experiment.

The review uses two complementary comparisons. Figure 7 organizes prediction methods by eight task goals, including temporal prediction, limited-data learning, lightweight deployment, and multimodal prediction. Table V restricts numerical comparison to regression models evaluated on SCUT-FBP5500 with publicly reported MAE, RMSE, and PCC. This keeps the broader method survey separate from the smaller set of results that share an evaluation basis.

Stimuli and measurement

Stimulus design determines which facial variables can be examined. Static 2D photographs are inexpensive and easy to standardize, but include capture conditions such as lighting and camera pose. Dynamic 2D clips retain expression and head movement over time. Static 3D stimuli expose depth and shape through reconstruction, scanning, or parametric synthesis; dynamic 3D adds controlled motion. A manipulation therefore needs both a changed variable and a specification of what remains fixed.

The measurements answer different questions. Questionnaire ratings define the judgment being modeled. Eye tracking records how observers sample facial information; EEG resolves the timing of neural responses; fMRI provides spatial information about activation. Table II compares these affordances and costs. Eye movements do not directly reveal the judgment, and high temporal resolution does not provide precise spatial localization. Combining modalities is useful when the analysis names what each measurement contributes.

The data-processing steps matter as much as the choice of instrument. Eye-tracking analysis requires calibration and checks for drift or missing samples before comparing fixation duration, scan paths, or regions of interest. EEG analysis removes artifacts and filters the signal before examining response timing. An early structural-encoding response such as N170 and a later evaluative response such as LPP are different observations. I keep those procedures attached to the reported finding, because otherwise a table can make “eye tracking” or “EEG” look like a single interchangeable label.

This distinction also affects the wording of a result. A trustworthiness score belongs to an observer sample judging particular stimuli under particular instructions. Predicting that score estimates the recorded impression. The stimulus, observer population, and task remain part of the result even when the final model takes only an image as input.

Comparing datasets before comparing models

Table III records stimulus format, dataset size, demographic coverage, trait labels, and measurement scales. Figure 4 places examples from twelve datasets together. The differences are substantial: SCUT-FBP5500 provides attractiveness ratings, while other resources include trustworthiness, dominance, binary attributes, or EEG-associated material. A dataset name alone does not specify the prediction problem.

For model selection, I separate the available input, the rating target, and the evaluation protocol. Natural images and controlled 3D stimuli expose different variation. A binary attribute and a mean rating require different losses. Identity overlap or shared capture conditions between training and test sets can also affect how a result should be interpreted. These checks belong beside the benchmark table, where the comparison is made.

For ratings, the unit of aggregation is another part of the protocol. Standardizing within each rater can reduce differences in how people use a scale before their responses are averaged. The resulting consensus is still tied to that observer group. Retaining the individual responses additionally allows the study to examine agreement, demographic variation, or a full rating distribution. These are different targets derived from the same collection process, so I record the aggregation rule alongside the nominal scale.

Rating distributions and model constraints

A mean score compresses the responses of several raters into one target. The prediction section therefore compares scalar regression with ranking objectives and label distribution learning. Ranking losses retain relative order; distribution targets retain information about how ratings spread. These objectives optimize different properties even when the reported output is an overall attractiveness score.

The loss function should follow the information retained in the annotation. Mean-squared error treats a rating as a point target; two faces with the same mean are equivalent even if observers strongly disagree about only one of them. A distribution objective can preserve that difference. A ranking objective asks which item should score higher, but does not by itself calibrate the numerical distance between them. Reading MAE, RMSE, and PCC together is therefore useful: absolute error, sensitivity to large errors, and correlation describe different aspects of a predictor.

Architecture introduces another constraint. Convolutional models have a local receptive bias; non-local attention and Transformer-based methods incorporate wider facial relationships such as symmetry. The reviewed Transformer and hybrid methods also rely on data augmentation and sufficient training material. Their accuracy must be read alongside the amount of data and training cost, especially when a study has only a small rated dataset.

The remaining methodological gaps concern dynamic behavior, demographic coverage, and the interpretation of component effects. Static averages leave out part of how an impression develops over time. A broader dataset or a more accurate predictor addresses only the variation it actually represents. The review’s comparison tables make those coverage differences explicit.

Tools, in context

Stimulus and measurement comparison
Compare static/dynamic and 2D/3D stimuli alongside questionnaire, eye-tracking, EEG, and fMRI measurements.
Dataset and benchmark tables
Record label construction and population coverage; restrict numerical comparisons to a shared task and evaluation dataset.
